# Published evidence overturns most Wane simulator guesses

Of the roughly twenty guessed parameters in `puffsim.py`, the published literature confirms two central values, a nicotine half-life near 2 h and a withdrawal-relevant tolerance time constant inside the 3 to 14 day window, and contradicts most of the rest in magnitude, spread or structure. Direct vaper data put the between-person spread of nicotine half-life at about 29 to 35 % CV (n = 13 and 33), not the simulated ±20 %; machine-aerosol measurements imply that nicotine yield grows at least in proportion to puff duration (a derived log-log slope of 1.14 to 1.36), not with an exponent of 0.7; adults on refillable devices puff for a median 3.1 to 3.4 s rather than 2.1 s, bouts under a 60 s gap rule average about 4 puffs rather than 8, in-bout gaps have a median of 13 s rather than 9.8 s, and day-to-day variation in puffs is two to three times larger than simulated. The largest mismatch is structural. Published craving predicts lapse through a shallow logistic (odds ratio about 1.9 per standardised unit, 237 studies, 51,788 participants) with no absolute threshold, the population relapse hazard falls roughly as 1/t from about 0.4 per day on quit day, and vapers compensate for a strength cut partially and persistently (total puff time ×1.50 and cotinine ×0.62 after a 67 % strength cut, n = 20), whereas the simulator's compensation fades as its tolerance state adapts. Outcome anchors are sobering: supported, drug-free cessation reaches about 13 to 20 % validated abstinence at the end of treatment or at 4 to 6 months, far below the 67 to 80 % success implied by the simulator's current target that "a fifth to a third fail a fixed ladder". Nearly every dynamic constant (tolerance, withdrawal, craving, relapse) comes from cigarette smokers who quit abruptly. No published study measures a months-long e-liquid taper, so compensation dynamics, per-step withdrawal, lapse-to-relapse progression with nicotine still available, and relapse after reaching zero remain for the Wane pilot to measure.

## How to read the numbers

Every value below is a published literature value, or arithmetic on published values, for parameterising synthetic people in a simulation that compares taper strategies. Nothing here is a statement about what any device should deliver to any person. Four flags recur. **Smoker-only** marks parameters measured in cigarette smokers (often by intravenous labelled nicotine) and never in vapers. **Industry** marks studies authored or funded by tobacco, e-cigarette or NRT manufacturers. **Derived** marks arithmetic done by the research team on published numbers (for example a fitted curve or a converted hazard); these are not published estimates and are labelled wherever they appear. **Internal** marks a per-person reanalysis of the open LSBU dataset ([Dawkins et al. 2018 data](https://doi.org/10.18744/LSBU.002952)) recorded in the Wane repository, which is not a peer-reviewed publication. Evidence strength in the consolidated table uses four grades: high (meta-analysis or several large consistent studies), moderate (one or two adequately sized studies, or consistent small ones), low (small n, single study, survivor-biased sample, or derived value), and very low (not identifiable from data, or analogy only).

The simulator's current values quoted throughout come from reading `puffsim.py` and `population.py` in the `wane-claude-perpuff` checkout: `HALF_LIFE_H = 2.0` with a per-person lognormal factor of σ 0.2; `DUR_EXP = 0.7`; tolerance time constant equal to 1/`craving_decay`, with `craving_decay` drawn uniformly from 0.08 to 0.25 per day (4 to 12.5 days) and clipped to 3 to 14 days; `S_FLOOR = 0.3` in W = max(0, S − C)/(S + 0.3); a daily relapse hazard of 0.0003 + 0.03 × logistic((x − θ)/0.015), where x is the 3-day mean of awake W above the same weekday's baseline and θ ranges from 0.08 to 0.17; elasticity e drawn from Normal(0.47, 0.25) and clipped to 0.05 to 0.85 inside `puffsim.py`; `DUR_S = 2.1` s; `IPI_S = 9.8` s; bout size 1 + Poisson(7); puffs/day log-uniform from 50 to 450; day-to-day noise σ 0.08 to 0.25 applied only in "irregular" runs; a weekend factor drawn from 1.0 to 2.2; and night bouts at a rate of 0.03 × W² per hour for everyone.

## Nicotine clears in about two hours, but people differ at least threefold

The central half-life survives scrutiny. In 13 experienced vapers who took 15 puffs on their own devices, **mean nicotine half-life was 106.3 min (SD 37.2, 95 % CI 84 to 129 min), with individual values from 36 to 152 min** ([St Helen et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4749433/)). The same laboratory found 124 to 151 min across e-liquids in 14 vapers ([St Helen et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5565733/)) and, within 33 dual users, 137.6 min (SD 39.3) after vaping against 121.2 min (SD 33.9) after a cigarette (p = 0.021), ranging from 125.1 min for cig-a-likes (n = 12) to 161.5 min for variable-power tanks (n = 6) ([St Helen et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7339816/)). The textbook smoker figure is "about 2 h" ([Benowitz, Hukkanen and Jacob 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2953858/)). All three vaper studies come from one group, so the central value is moderately rather than strongly established.

The spread is where the simulator fails. A lognormal σ of 0.2 puts two thirds of synthetic people within roughly ×0.82 to ×1.22 of 2 h, but the direct vaper SDs are 35 % of the mean in 13 experienced vapers and 29 % in 33 dual users, and the observed individuals run from 66 % below to 43 % above the mean. Smoker studies with intravenous labelled nicotine identify the sources. By CYP2A6 genotype, **mean clearance was 18.8 ± 6.0, 15.5 ± 4.9 and 11.7 ± 5.1 mL/min/kg in the full, about 80 % and about 50 % activity groups** (n = 278 twins, mostly white) ([Benowitz et al. 2006a](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/17112802)). By nicotine metabolite ratio quartile, **half-life was 164 (SD 36), 141 (22), 128 (22) and 98 (15) min** in 60 African American smokers ([Benowitz et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4892970/)). Clearance was 18.8 ± 6.6 mL/min/kg in women against 15.6 ± 4.3 in men, and 22.5 ± 6.6 in oral contraceptive users against 17.6 ± 6.1 in non-users (n = 294) ([Benowitz et al. 2006b](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/16678549)). Population models give 27 % CV on clearance across 930 smokers ([Olsson Gisleskog et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8016787/), industry, Johnson and Johnson affiliates) and 68 % inter-individual variability in apparent clearance across 702 smokers ([Marchand et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5681983/), industry, Philip Morris International). Stacking sex, contraceptive use and genotype gives a span of at least threefold in a mixed population (derived), all smoker-only; no study has measured the metabolite ratio alongside vaping pharmacokinetics. Menthol is a genuine conflict: a crossover in 14 smokers found clearance of 1289 against 1431 mL/min with mentholated cigarettes (p = 0.02) ([Benowitz, Herrera and Jacob 2004](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/15084646)), while the industry population model found no effect on AUC ([Marchand et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5681983/)).

A single exponential also misdescribes the first half hour. Disposition studies resolve a **distribution half-life of about 8 min** ([Benowitz, Hukkanen and Jacob 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2953858/)), and a three-compartment fit to 930 smokers places half-lives of 7 min, 57 min and 4.5 h on 4 %, 36 % and 61 % of the AUC ([Olsson Gisleskog et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8016787/), industry). Terminal half-lives of 11 h from urinary excretion and 17 h from a 702-smoker population model describe a slow low-concentration tail that matters mainly for overnight residual levels ([Marchand et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5681983/), industry). Clearance also rises about 40 % for 30 to 60 min after a meal (smoker-only) ([Benowitz, Hukkanen and Jacob 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2953858/)). Venous sampling understates what reaches the brain: arterial peaks averaged twice venous peaks in 12 male smokers ([Gourlay and Benowitz 1997](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/9357397)), and PET imaging in 23 smokers showed brain nicotine at half-maximum 19 ± 3 s after a puff in non-dependent and 33 ± 4 s in dependent smokers ([Rose et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2841893/)), with a comparable 27 s after a single e-cigarette puff in 17 vapers ([Solingapuram Sai et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8008769/), senior author discloses grants from JUUL Labs, Philip Morris International and Altria). The same PET work found that at inter-puff intervals of 15 to 90 s brain concentration rises monotonically through a session with no puff-by-puff peaks, which supports integrating puffs into a smooth level rather than modelling each puff as a separate spike. Perceived urge relief across six vaporised products tracked peak concentration (R² = .86, p = .007) but not time to peak (R² = .0035) in 18 smokers ([Voos et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6646880/)).

The delivery exponent of 0.7 is contradicted in direction. In machine-generated aerosol from one cartomizer, 15 puffs yielded **0.11, 0.30 and 0.72 mg of nicotine at puff durations of 2, 4 and 8 s at 3.3 V, and 0.64, 1.18 and 3.23 mg at 5.2 V**, while puff flow rate had no detectable effect (p = 0.12 to 0.97) ([Talih et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4837998/)); a 100-condition validation confirmed that flow rate does not drive yield (r = 0.85 between modelled and measured flux) ([Talih et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5502764/)). A log-log fit to those published points gives slopes of 1.31 to 1.36 at 3.3 V and 1.14 to 1.17 at 5.2 V (derived; no paper reports an exponent). In users, puff duration was the only topography variable correlated with post-bout plasma nicotine (r = .44 to .56 across three strengths) in 33 experienced vapers and 31 naive smokers ([Hiler et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5657238/)). Three durations on one cartomizer cannot distinguish a power law from a saturating curve, and transfer to pod hardware is untested. Loss by exhalation is negligible: retention was 93.8 % in 13 vapers and 98.6 to 99.2 % in 14 ([St Helen et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4749433/); [St Helen et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5565733/)). Between-person variation in delivery rivals variation in elimination: within one product, Cmax CVs of 50 to 108 % were reported in 15 smokers ([O'Connell et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6722145/), industry, Imperial Brands), and 6.1 ± 5.5 ng/mL in 33 dual users ([St Helen et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7339816/)). Because `puffsim.py` normalises each person's baseline daily mean level to 1, the absolute milligrams per puff cancel out, but the within-person shape of delivery against duration does not.

## Tolerance, withdrawal and craving run on three different clocks

`puffsim.py` uses one state S with one time constant, and it sets that constant from `craving_decay`, so tolerance, withdrawal resolution and craving decay are the same number. The literature separates them. The withdrawal syndrome is fast: symptoms rise within 30 to 180 min of the last dose in 50 smokers in the laboratory ([Hendricks et al. 2006](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/16752139)), peak on days 1 to 3, and resolve within 10 days by ecological momentary assessment in 214 quitters ([Shiffman et al. 2006](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/17156182)) or within 2 to 4 weeks by the judgement of a 120-study review ([Hughes 2007](https://downloads.regulations.gov/FDA-2024-N-5471-0075/content.pdf)). Fitting the simulator's own W = S/(S + 0.3) to those endpoints gives **τ ≈ 3 d for "baseline by day 10" and τ ≈ 5 to 8 d for "2 to 4 weeks"** (derived), so the current 3 to 14 day window holds but the evidence centres near 5 d. Vapers follow the same inverted U: among 59 compliant former-smoker daily vapers (of 109 enrolled) abstinent for 6 days, withdrawal peaked on days 1 to 2 and rose by **0.41 points (95 % CI 0.29 to 0.54) on a 0 to 3 scale, against 0.55 in abstinent smokers**, about three quarters of the smoker amplitude ([Hughes et al. 2020a](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7171279/)); in 30 never-smoker vapers the rise was smaller still ([Hughes et al. 2020b](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/31504882)). Durations conflict: 10 days by EMA against 31 days or more for depression, tension, irritability and appetite in 96 abstinent women ([Gilbert et al. 2002](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/11860040)), and 38 % and 61 % of quitters in two patch trials did not follow the typical rise-then-fall shape ([Piasecki, Fiore and Baker 1998](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/9604553), as summarised by [Hughes 2007](https://downloads.regulations.gov/FDA-2024-N-5471-0075/content.pdf)).

A slower receptor process sits underneath. SPECT imaging of 19 smokers against 20 never-smokers showed β2* receptor availability **about 22 % above non-smokers in striatum after 1 week of abstinence, still 14 to 18 % above at 4 weeks, and normal by 6 to 12 weeks** ([Cosgrove et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2796827/)); another group measured 26 to 36 % excess in cortex at 6.8 ± 1.9 days ([Staley et al. 2006](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/16928859)); a third, with 10 smokers, saw 25.7 ± 9.2 % excess at 10 days and normal values by 21 days ([Mamede et al. 2007](https://jnm.snmjournals.org/content/48/11/1829)). A single exponential through the Cosgrove means gives τ ≈ 45 to 50 d, while Mamede implies under 10 d (derived; cross-sectional, 6 to 19 people per time point, smoker-only). Receptor excess correlated with craving (ρ = 0.74 at 4 weeks) and with urge to smoke, but not with withdrawal severity, which points to the slow process driving craving and relapse vulnerability rather than the acute syndrome. Partial reduction produces partial normalisation: 11 weeks of treatment in 48 smokers produced a mean 58.2 % normalisation, graded with the fall in cigarettes per day ([Brody et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3682149/)), which supports the simulator's assumption that S tracks the nicotine level continuously. Nothing measures how fast tolerance is re-acquired when intake rises, and no receptor study exists in exclusive vapers.

A third, very fast clock governs acute tolerance. Intravenous nicotine in 8 volunteers gave **a half-life of 35 min for both the development and the regression of acute tolerance to heart rate, with up to 80 % loss of effect** ([Porchet, Benowitz and Sheiner 1988](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/3336000)); subjective tolerance to nasal spray in 16 smokers mostly did not dissipate within 2 h ([Perkins et al. 1995](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/7617803)). Overnight abstinence resensitises considerably ([Benowitz 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2928221/)). A one-state model with a τ of days cannot produce a strongest first puff of the morning.

The floor constant k = 0.3 is not identifiable. For a floor effect, the gradual arm of a 20-week trial in 1,250 smokers showed no withdrawal spike at any 4-weekly step, including the final 2.4 to 0.4 mg/g step, whereas an immediate cut raised week-1 withdrawal to 9.33 against 7.03 in controls on the 0 to 32 MNWS scale (adjusted difference 2.39, 95 % CI 1.68 to 3.10) ([Hatsukami et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6372240/)). Against it, after 6 weeks on very low nicotine cigarettes an 18 h abstinence test produced withdrawal no different from normal-nicotine smokers in 840 randomised smokers ([Donny et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4642683/)). The two trials also disagree on whether an abrupt cut to 0.4 mg/g raises week-1 withdrawal at all (not significant with about 120 per arm, significant with about 400 per arm).

The dose-response shape of withdrawal against a cut has never been mapped across three or more blinded levels. Two strands of evidence argue against a purely linear deficit. Receptor occupancy saturates: **50 % α4β2* occupancy at 0.75 to 0.87 ng/mL venous nicotine** in smokers (n = 11) ([Brody et al. 2006](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/16894067); [Brody et al. 2009](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/18706128)), against plasma levels of 12.9 ng/mL after the first and 17.3 ng/mL after the third cigarette of the day in 136 smokers ([Liakoni et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7967935/)), so any receptor-level signal is strongly concave in concentration. And urge saturates earlier than symptoms: in 20 experienced vapers, a week at 6 against 18 mg/mL cut salivary cotinine from 402.52 to 250.45 ng/mL (fixed power) and raised strength of urges from 2.15 to 2.90 on a 0 to 5 scale, with withdrawal scores of 9.05 against 7.80 ([Dawkins et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6150437/)), an urge rise comparable to the +0.68 seen with full abstinence in Hughes 2020a; in 60 smokers, 24 h abstinence moved craving (η² 0.45 for QSU-brief) about twice as much as symptom totals (η² 0.22 for MNWS) ([West and Ussher 2010](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/20033133)). The Dawkins comparison was unblinded to strength, so expectancy could inflate the urge effect. Overnight abstinence behaves like 6 h of daytime abstinence: median plasma nicotine 1.0 ng/mL, QSU 4 and MNWS 7 after the night against 1.4 ng/mL, QSU 3.85 and MNWS 8 after 6 daytime hours in 136 smokers ([Liakoni et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7967935/)); the simulator's W gives 0.72 against 0.67 for those two cases (derived), which already matches.

Craving decays on a longer tail than withdrawal. Among 52 clinic abstainers on nicotine gum, **35 % still had strong urges at week 4** ([West, Hajek and Belcher 1987](https://doi.org/10.1016/0306-4603(87)90028-1)). In 452 quitters with behavioural support, urge strength among CO-validated abstainers followed an exponential decline over 52 weeks, with **13 % reporting strong urges at 6 months (66 abstainers assessed) and 34 % some urges but none strong at 12 months (38 assessed)** ([Ussher et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-012-2886-7)), a survivor-biased sample. Among 403 ex-smokers abstinent 1 to 10 years, 59 % reported some desire to smoke in the past year and 11 % at a clinically significant level ([Hughes 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2902924/)). A cross-study calculation from 35 % strong urges at week 4 to 13 % at week 26 gives a slow-component half-life of about 15 weeks (derived, weak). A single 3 to 14 day time constant makes simulated craving vanish by week 3 or 4, which these data contradict. Heterogeneity is large: in 935 treatment-seeking smokers, day-4 urge averaged 2.8 on a 1 to 4 scale with a between-person SD of 0.70 and a slope SD of 0.0997 per day, and urge trajectory raised relapse-prediction AUC from 0.594 to 0.670 ([Javitz, Lerman and Swan 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3394903/)). Quit-day withdrawal classes in 1,236 smokers were moderate 64 %, high craving plus anhedonia 8 %, affective 13 % and hunger 15 %, with 8-week abstinence of 50 %, 35 %, 43 % and 48 % ([Piper et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.13613)). Pharmacotherapy lowered withdrawal severity but did not change its slope or volatility in 893 trial smokers ([Piasecki et al. 2003c](https://doi.org/10.1037/1064-1297.11.4.276)). Diurnal shape conflicts between studies: during ordinary smoking craving is highest on waking (2.8 % above the person mean in 351 heavy smokers) ([Chandra, Scharf and Shiffman 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3123683/)), but during enforced abstinence it is lowest in the morning and builds through the day (209 smokers) ([Perkins et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2734287/)).

## Relapse falls roughly as one over time, with no sharp threshold

The simulator's hazard is a steep logistic: its odds multiply by e^(1/0.015), about ×7.4 for every 0.03 of excess W. The published relationship between craving and subsequent use is shallow. A meta-analysis of 237 studies (656 statistics, 51,788 participants, all substances) found **prospective odds ratios of 1.87 (95 % CI 1.73 to 2.02) in nicotine studies, 2.05 in EMA studies, 2.37 for same-day prediction and 3.01 for cue-induced craving, falling to 1.31 after trim-and-fill adjustment** ([Vafaie and Kober 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9161117/)). Those ORs were converted from effect sizes; read as per standardised unit of craving they give a logistic slope of about 0.64 (range 0.27 to 1.10) per within-person SD (derived). The simulator's slope of 66.7 per unit of W equals the pooled estimate only if the within-person SD of the 3-day mean excess W is about 0.0096 (derived: 0.64 ÷ 66.7); any larger spread makes the simulated hazard steeper than every pooled estimate. There is no absolute threshold: in 137 women who relapsed or not over 30 days, within-person craving peaked on the relapse day at **+1.4 SD after a ramp over the previous 2 to 5 days**, and the authors note "the lack of an absolute symptom threshold" ([Allen et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1080/14622200701705076)). Only 47 % of 198 craving-outcome associations across 62 cessation studies were significant, and craving is "not a necessary condition of relapse" ([Wray, Gass and Tiffany 2013](https://doi.org/10.1093/ntr/nts268)). The craving effect grows with time: in 1,296 ITC ex-smokers, urge frequency did not predict relapse in month 1 (OR 1.09, 0.83 to 1.43) but did afterwards (OR 1.42, 1.25 to 1.60) ([Herd, Borland and Hyland 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4517970/)). Across more than 2,600 smokers, more than 32 % with high post-cessation craving relapsed within 1 week against fewer than 15 % with low craving ([Killen and Fortmann 1997](https://doi.org/10.1037/1064-1297.5.2.137)).

The shape over time is steeply front-loaded. In the ITC cohort, conditional 1-year relapse fell with abstinence duration at baseline: **78 % (114/146) at 1 to 7 days, 64 % (154/239) at 8 to 30 days, 42 % (274/655) at 31 to 182 days, 22 % (65/291) at 183 to 365 days, 17 % (54/326) at 1 to 2 years and 5 % (7/143) beyond 2 years** ([Herd, Borland and Hyland 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4517970/)). A binomial fit gives h(t) ≈ 0.40/(t + 1)^1.05 per day with t in days, which implies 0.045 per day at day 7, 0.011 at day 30 and 0.0008 at day 365, and 36 % survival at day 14 (derived; mixes aided and unaided quitters). That matches the 62 % relapse by 2 weeks observed in 235 self-quitters ([Garvey et al. 1992](https://doi.org/10.1016/0306-4603(92)90042-t)), and a review of self-quitters found most relapse in the first 8 days and only 3 to 5 % prolonged abstinence at 6 to 12 months ([Hughes, Keely and Naud 2004](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1360-0443.2004.00540.x)). This population curve is steeper than any one person's hazard because the most vulnerable leave first, so it serves as a calibration target for a simulated abrupt quit, not as a per-person hazard. The simulator's ceiling of 0.0303 per day sits far below the 0.09 to 0.40 per day that the fit implies for days 0 to 3 of an abrupt stop.

The background rate of 0.0003 per day is right for one period only. After a year of abstinence, **annual relapse was 10 % (95 % CI 5 to 17 %)** across 8 RCTs of nicotine medication ([Hughes, Peters and Naud 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.addbeh.2008.05.012)), which converts to 0.00029 per day (derived); it was 2 to 4 % per year in years 2 to 6 and below 1 % per year after 10 years in 483 men followed up to 35 years ([Krall, Garvey and Garcia 2002](https://doi.org/10.1080/14622200110098428)); 37.1 % of 1,578 one-year abstainers were estimated to relapse within 10 years ([Hawkins, Hollingworth and Campbell 2010](https://doi.org/10.1093/ntr/ntq175)). In the months after treatment ends, hazards are 5 to 20 times higher. Across 61 RCTs of first-line drugs (27,647 participants), sustained abstinence on NRT fell from 34.8 % at 3 months to 26.6 % at 6 and 19.8 % at 12 ([Rosen et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.14134)), about 0.29 % per day in months 3 to 6 and 0.16 % per day in months 6 to 12 (derived, constant-hazard assumption). In vaping trials, continuous abstinence declined after active drug stopped by about 0.2 to 1.1 % per day in the following 1 to 3 months (derived from [Caponnetto et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-023-02919-2), n = 140; [Rigotti et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2024.1313), n = 160; [Evins et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2025.3810), n = 261 aged 16 to 25). All relapse constants above are smoker-only except the vaping-trial arithmetic.

Lapse and relapse are separate processes. Among 235 self-quitters, those who smoked at all after quitting had **a 95 % probability of resuming regular smoking** ([Garvey et al. 1992](https://doi.org/10.1016/0306-4603(92)90042-t)); in two patch trials (n = 88 and 112), 83 % and 97 % of those smoking in week 2 were smoking at 6 months ([Kenford et al. 1994](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.271.8.589)). Progression is gradual: among 203 EMA-monitored lapsers, the median time to first lapse was 3.8 days, and the 45 % who resumed daily smoking did so after a median of about 13 days and 5 lapses; each drop in self-efficacy after a lapse raised the hazard of the next (HR 1.09, 1.02 to 1.15), and the nicotine patch slowed progression (HR 0.58, 0.48 to 0.70) ([Kirchner, Shiffman and Wileyto 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3296289/)); a 35 mg patch had its largest effect on lapse-to-relapse progression (HR 7.1) in 324 smokers ([Shiffman et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.74.2.276)). Episodic cues rival tonic craving. In 92 disadvantaged quitters, EMA reports within 4 h before a first lapse, compared with abstainers' reports, endorsed urge 59.3 % against 32.8 %, stress 41.1 % against 25.9 %, alcohol 19.1 % against 3.4 % and someone smoking nearby 33.6 % against 12.1 % ([Businelle et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.2196/jmir.6307)); easy availability characterised almost 75 % of 551 early lapses ([Deiches et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1093/ntr/ntt074)); and within-day rises in negative affect over hours, not day-level stress, preceded lapses in 215 quitters ([Shiffman and Waters 2004](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.72.2.192)). For Wane users the device is always available, so a lapse means use above plan and relapse means returning to the pre-taper level or abandoning the taper; no study measures lapse-to-relapse under those conditions. Unassisted vaping quit attempts fail fastest of all: in a quit app, 78 % of 468 challenges by 360 users relapsed by day 2 ([Nathwani et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.18332/tid/224436)), and median abstinence was 2.19 against 0.68 days for high against low self-efficacy in 389 users ([Tian et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.addbeh.2026.108754)).

## Vapers recover a third to a half of a strength cut, and keep doing so

The best measurement of compensation in real use is the LSBU study: 20 experienced UK vapers (mean age 37.9) spent a week each at 6 and 18 mg/mL, first at a fixed 10 W and then with adjustable power. At fixed power, **puffs rose from 279 ± 127 to 338 ± 161 per day, puff duration from 3.61 ± 0.97 to 4.46 ± 1.22 s, and liquid from 4.63 ± 2.13 to 6.19 ± 3.74 mL/day, yet salivary cotinine still fell from 402.52 ± 190.00 to 250.45 ± 188.23 ng/mL** ([Dawkins et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6150437/)). That is total puff time ×1.50, liquid ×1.34 and cotinine ×0.62, so the linear fraction of lost nicotine recovered was 0.25 by puff time, 0.17 by liquid and 0.43 by cotinine (derived). With adjustable power, 13 of 19 raised voltage by a mean 0.5 V, puff duration no longer rose (3.81 against 3.91 s), and cotinine was ×0.68. Order effects were significant for cotinine, so single-week contrasts are noisy. Per person, total puffing rose by a median ×1.47 (IQR 1.34 to 1.68, range 0.93 to 2.15), a log-elasticity ε (where puffing = r^(−ε) for strength ratio r) with median 0.35, IQR 0.27 to 0.47 and range −0.07 to 0.70 (internal; derived from the open data). About 5 % of people showed no compensation or a small reversal.

Acute laboratory studies agree on partial recovery. In 11 experienced men vaping ad libitum for 60 min, liquid consumption roughly doubled at 6 against 24 mg/mL, but plasma boost was 22.03 against 43.57 ng/mL at 60 min, a recovered fraction of 0.01 at 10 min, 0.31 at 30 min and 0.34 at 60 min (derived) ([Dawkins et al. 2016](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27235016)). In a crossover of 50 US pod users aged 21 to 35, lower-strength pods (35 or 28 mg/mL against 59 mg/mL) raised median puff duration from 2.4 to 2.6 s and total inhaled volume from 1,490 to 1,990 mL with unchanged flow, while plasma boost was 0.0043 against 0.0060 mg/L (n = 30), a recovered fraction of 0.30 to 0.45 (derived); participants with moderate to high dependence showed a 42 % larger puff-duration response (ratio 1.42, 1.12 to 1.80) ([Ferdous et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11282440/)). Across 27 pharmacokinetic studies (2,058 subjects with baseline-adjusted Cmax), plasma peak rose by only 0.21 ng/mL per mg/mL of e-liquid (r = 0.15), because users adjust their puffing ([Jacobson et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7786013/), industry, Japan Tobacco International).

Over months, voluntary reductions look fully compensated. Among 98 exclusive vapers followed for 8 months, median strength fell from 11 to 6 mg/mL and liquid rose from 80 to 100 mL/month while cotinine was unchanged (252 against 307 ng/mL, p = 0.4) ([Etter 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2016.01.003)); among 27 vapers over 12 months, strength fell from 13.83 to 9.91 mg/mL, liquid rose from 4.44 to 6.84 mL/day and cotinine stayed at 370.88 against 415.78 ng/mL ([Soar et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.addbeh.2018.07.019)). Both samples chose their own reductions, so selection explains part of the contrast with imposed cuts. The cigarette literature brackets the vaping values. When yield is cut by filter ventilation with nicotine still in the tobacco, smokers recover most of it: 78 % by cotinine for a 50 % yield cut ([Benowitz et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1158/1055-9965.EPI-04-0667)) and a compensation index of 0.63 to 0.78 across brand-switching and cross-sectional studies ([Scherer and Lee 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.yrtph.2014.09.008), funding not verified). When nicotine is removed from the tobacco itself, compensation is small and transient: across 7 US trials (2,454 smokers) of very low nicotine cigarettes, 22.9 % were predicted to raise cigarettes per day at week 1 and 0.8 % at week 6 ([Denlinger-Apte et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13373663/)); in a 6-month monthly taper in 135 smokers, cotinine held steady for 6 weeks, then fell to 44 % of baseline by week 26, with log compensation near complete at the first step, 40 to 60 % at middle levels and 20 to 40 % at the lowest ([Benowitz et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3348427/)). An e-liquid strength cut resembles the first case more than the second, since more liquid always supplies more nicotine.

Timing is where `puffsim.py` departs most. In the simulator, compensation is a gain on W, and because S follows C, W and therefore compensation shrink as tolerance adapts over its 3 to 14 day constant (reading of the code). In vapers the opposite was seen: at fixed power, the daily puffing-time excess at low strength **kept rising from day 1 to day 5** (days 3, 4 and 5 each greater than day 1, p < .05), carried mainly by puff duration, and stayed flat under adjustable power (n = 19) ([Cox et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8186419/)). A secondary analysis of 129 participants across 11 conditions found first-puff duration already differed by condition before nicotine could reach the brain, with no further within-session increase in low-flux conditions, which the authors read as open-loop sensory regulation ([Talih et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2026.113120)). No study follows an imposed vaping cut beyond one week. The split between channels fits the simulator's hard-case setting: the duration share of the log increase was 0.52 from LSBU group means ([Dawkins et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6150437/)), 0.58 from per-person medians (internal), 0.47 after a power cut from 10 to 6 W in 21 vapers ([Farsalinos et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1093/ntr/ntx219)) and about 0.35 in pods ([Ferdous et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11282440/)); the default `dur_share` of 0.4 sits at the low end, and 0.57 sits at the top.

## Real puffing is longer, burstier and noisier than simulated

Puffs per day are roughly right in the centre and wrong in shape. In the largest device log, 185 users of a connected eGo-type device recorded 1,000,000 puffs over 6,144 user-days: **mean 163 ± 138 puffs/day, median 132, with half of all days between 55 and 236 and fewer than 1 % above 600** ([Dautzenberg and Bricard 2015](https://www.omicsonline.org/open-access/realtime-characterization-of-ecigarettes-use-the-1-million-puffs-study-2155-6105-1000229.php?aid=54615), published in an OMICS journal with weak peer review). A puff-recording device in 58 UK adults logged about 250 puffs/day from days 2 to 3 onward, with single days from 5 to more than 400 ([Gao, Humberstone and Liu 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9947749/)); 24 adults over 24 h averaged 156.2 ± 95.3 (range 44 to 345) ([Kosmider et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6377077/)); and the 20 LSBU vapers had a median of 292 (114 to 585) at 18 mg/mL ([Dawkins et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.14271)). The simulated log-uniform 50 to 450 range brackets these medians but gives no mass to the heavy tail up to about 600. New devices take time to settle: the eGo cohort fell from 209 to 127 puffs/day over its first 60 days while mean puff duration rose from 3.40 to 4.11 s, and the PR-ENDS cohort logged about half its plateau on day 1, with puff duration stabilising after 5 to 7 days.

The simulated puff duration of 2.1 s comes from 20 young adults (18 to 22 years, 19 men) on first-generation cig-a-likes ([Robinson et al. 2016](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0164038)). Adults on refillable devices puff longer: **median 3.10 s (mean 3.44 ± 1.65 s) in 58 UK adults** ([Gao, Humberstone and Liu 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9947749/)) and **median 3.40 s (mean 3.79 ± 1.89 s) across 1,000,000 puffs** ([Dautzenberg and Bricard 2015](https://www.omicsonline.org/open-access/realtime-characterization-of-ecigarettes-use-the-1-million-puffs-study-2155-6105-1000229.php?aid=54615)). Pod users are closer to the simulated value: 2.18 ± 1.00 s among 57 adults aged 18 to 47 (79 % on fourth-generation pods) ([Dowd et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13396144/)). Puff-to-puff variation is larger than simulated: a CV of about 0.48 to 0.50 in both device logs (derived) against a per-puff lognormal σ of 0.3, while daily mean puff duration is steadier, with day-to-day CVs mostly between 0 and 40 % ([Gao, Humberstone and Liu 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9947749/)).

Bouts are small and overdispersed. Under the only gap rule with published real-world statistics (a new bout after 60 s without a puff), eGo users averaged **4.02 ± 5.55 puffs per series including isolated puffs**, and 9.28 % of all puffs were isolated, 31.35 % in series of 2 to 5, 36.68 % in series of 6 to 15 and 22.68 % in series longer than 15 ([Dautzenberg and Bricard 2015](https://www.omicsonline.org/open-access/realtime-characterization-of-ecigarettes-use-the-1-million-puffs-study-2155-6105-1000229.php?aid=54615)). Of 3,581 pod puffs video-coded over 4 h, 19.4 % were unclustered, 45.9 % in short clusters (mean 2.57), 21.2 % in medium (mean 7.07) and 13.6 % in long clusters (mean 14.41) ([Dowd et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13396144/)), which works out to about 2.43 puffs per bout and roughly 47 % single-puff bouts (derived). The simulator's 1 + Poisson(7) has mean 8, variance 7 and a single-puff probability of e^(−7), about 0.1 %, whereas the eGo data have a variance-to-mean ratio of about 7.7 (derived). The published "median 7 puffs per series" in the same eGo paper is probably puff-weighted, and a coarser "session" level (gaps of minutes) gives 10.2 ± 7.9 puffs per session and 15.3 ± 8.0 sessions/day in 24 adults ([Kosmider et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6377077/)), so the current mean of 8 matches sessions rather than 60 s bouts. Within bouts, **the median inter-puff interval was 13.0 s (mean 16.56 ± 13.50 s)**, shortening from 19.26 ± 15.12 s in 2 to 5 puff series to 13.68 ± 11.53 s in series longer than 15 ([Dautzenberg and Bricard 2015](https://www.omicsonline.org/open-access/realtime-characterization-of-ecigarettes-use-the-1-million-puffs-study-2155-6105-1000229.php?aid=54615)); the SD-to-mean ratio of 0.82 implies a lognormal σ(ln) near 0.7 (derived) against the simulated 0.5. The simulated 9.8 s median does not appear in the published text of Robinson 2016, according to a targeted full-text check, and that sample is young adults on cig-a-likes.

Day-to-day noise is far larger than simulated. In the eGo cohort, between-user differences explained 41.3 % of the variance in puffs/day, leaving 58.7 % within users ([Dautzenberg and Bricard 2015](https://www.omicsonline.org/open-access/realtime-characterization-of-ecigarettes-use-the-1-million-puffs-study-2155-6105-1000229.php?aid=54615)), a pooled within-person CV of about 0.65 (derived; an upper bound inflated by adoption drift). In 58 PR-ENDS users, **per-person CVs of daily puff numbers ranged from 0 to 160 %** ([Gao, Humberstone and Liu 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9947749/)). The simulated σ of 0.08 to 0.25, applied only in irregular runs, sits below most of that range. The weekend effect runs the other way from the simulator's factor of 1.0 to 2.2: across the eGo cohort, **weekend puffs/day were 4.60 % lower than Monday to Wednesday**, with 00:00 to 03:00 puffs up 34.9 % and 06:00 to 10:00 puffs down 30.1 % (all p < 0.001), though individual social or alcohol-driven users could still be weekend-heavy. Use is roughly flat from 10:00 to 22:00 with a trough near 05:00; a 24 h study of 24 adults found peaks of 14.4 ± 14.9 puffs/h at 11:00 and 13.1 ± 3.4 at 18:00 ([Kosmider et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6377077/)). In 351 smokers, within-day patterns clustered as flatline 70 %, morning high 12 %, daily decline 9 % and evening incline 9 % ([Chandra et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/1064-1297.15.1.67)); no vaper equivalent exists.

Morning latency and night waking have published vaper distributions. Among 156 US adult daily exclusive vapers in PATH Wave 1 (2013 to 2014, 96.3 % on rechargeable devices), **15.1 % first vaped within 5 min of waking, 49.4 % at 6 to 30 min, 24.6 % at 31 to 60 min and 10.9 % after 60 min, a geometric mean of 29.2 min (95 % CI 24.4 to 34.9)** against 20.0 min in 3,430 daily smokers ([Liu et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5868349)); in 832 ITC England vapers in 2016, more than 40 % first vaped more than 60 min after waking ([Lohner et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.16162)), a conflict probably reflecting population and year. Night waking is a minority trait, not a universal fraction: among 494 US adult long-term vapers, **7.1 % of exclusive and 10.2 % of poly users woke at night to vape**, rising to 9.5 % and 15.3 % after 3.7 years, with a mean of 0.3 ± 1.2 nights/week across all exclusive users ([Du et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9811611/)), which implies about 4.2 nights/week among those who do wake (derived). Night smoking is far more common in treatment-seeking smokers: 51.1 % of 2,312 clinic patients ([Bover et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18199277/)), 41 % of 691 heavy smokers with electronic diaries, on 26 % of nights and about 2 episodes per night ([Scharf, Dunbar and Shiffman 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18188757/)), and 22.4 % of 2,884 Austrian programme patients ([Riemerth, Kunze and Groman 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19225735/)). In 35 young adult vapers, more wake time after sleep onset predicted higher next-day craving (β = 3.08, p = .028) ([Nam et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/ntr/ntaf056)). No study reports how night waking changes during a nicotine reduction.

## Supported tapers succeed for about one person in six at six months

The simulator's implicit target, that 20 to 33 % fail a fixed ladder (from the Wane research brief, `docs/research_brief_for_chatgpt.md`), is out of line with every trial anchor. Reducing first and quitting abruptly give **the same long-term abstinence (RR 1.01, 95 % CI 0.87 to 1.17) across 22 RCTs and 9,219 smokers** ([Lindson et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD013183.pub2)). In the largest head-to-head, 697 English primary-care smokers with nurse support and NRT in both arms, a 75 % reduction over 2 weeks gave validated prolonged abstinence of **39.2 % against 49.0 % at 4 weeks (RR 0.80) and 15.5 % against 22.0 % at 6 months (RR 0.71)**, and those who preferred gradual quitting did worse regardless of arm (38.3 % against 52.2 % at 4 weeks) ([Lindson-Hawley et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.7326/M14-2805)). Self-selection compounds this: smokers who preferred abrupt quitting succeeded at 16 % (n = 2,456) against 7 % for those who preferred gradual (n = 1,801) in an internet study ([Etter 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2011.04.016)), and cold-turkey attempts succeeded at 22 to 27 % against 12 to 16 % for cutting down in the ITC cohort of more than 8,000 smokers ([Cheong et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1080/14622200701484961)). People who choose a taper product are drawn from the lower-success group. In smokers not trying to quit, NRT-assisted reduction over 6 to 18 months produced 6.75 % sustained 6-month abstinence, about twice placebo (RR 2.06, 7 trials, n = 2,767) ([Moore et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.b1024)).

Vaping cessation trials set the vaper anchors. With placebo plus counselling, continuous abstinence was **20.0 % in weeks 4 to 12 and 17.2 % in weeks 4 to 24** in 140 Italian exclusive vapers ([Caponnetto et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-023-02919-2)), and **15.1 % in weeks 9 to 12 and 13.2 % in weeks 9 to 16** in 160 US adult daily vapers ([Rigotti et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2024.1313)); the active drugs roughly doubled these (varenicline 40.0 % and 34.3 %; cytisinicline 31.8 % and 23.4 %). Among 261 young people aged 16 to 25, abstinence in weeks 9 to 12 was 51 %, 14 % and 6 % with varenicline plus support, placebo plus support and text-only usual care, falling to 28 %, 7 % and 4 % by week 24 ([Evins et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2025.3810)). The only randomised evidence on an e-liquid nicotine taper is one pilot in which 6 of 8 young vapers in the taper arm reported being vape-free at 6 months, with no biochemical verification and unequal contact ([Sahr et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/pharmacy9010021)); the 2025 Cochrane living review rates it at very low certainty (RR 3.38, 0.43 to 26.30, 17 participants) across 15 RCTs and 5,800 participants in total ([Butler et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD016058.pub3)). Base rates outside trials are also low. Of 1,988 US adult established vapers in PATH Wave 4, 60.7 % planned to quit and 15.2 % had tried in the past year (7.9 % of former smokers) ([Palmer et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2021.4146)); of 912 exclusive adult vapers, 33.4 % had quit about 2 years later, 22.2 % of daily users and 26.8 % of former smokers (self-report) ([Dai and Young 2025](https://doi.org/10.1136/tc-2024-059098)); among 2,251 Southern California young adults, 47.9 % of those who had ever quit successfully were vaping again ([Whaley et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/ntr/ntaf148)). Ex-smokers who switch rarely leave nicotine: 80 % of 1-year smoking abstainers in the e-cigarette arm of an 886-person UK trial were still vaping at 52 weeks ([Hajek et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1808779)).

The only nicotine taper speeds tested head to head come from reduced-nicotine cigarettes. In the 1,250-smoker trial, the gradual arm stepped from 15.5 to 11.7, 5.2, 2.4 and 0.4 mg/g at 4-week intervals, cuts of 25 %, 56 %, 54 % and 83 % per step (derived); **20-week completion was 81 % against 68 % for an immediate cut and 86 % for controls**, and 69 % of gradual completers (57 % by intention to treat) were biomarker-adherent at the final step, while cigarettes per day (19.97 against 20.45) and cigarette-free days did not differ from controls ([Hatsukami et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6372240/)). Behavioural effects appeared only at or below about 15 % of normal content: at week 6, cigarettes per day were 20.8 at 5.2 mg/g but 16.5, 16.3 and 14.9 at 2.4, 1.3 and 0.4 mg/g against 21.3 at 15.8 mg/g ([Donny et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4642683/)). Tapering the NRT patch at the end of treatment was no better than stopping it (RR 0.99, 0.74 to 1.32, 2 studies, 264 smokers) ([Theodoulou et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10278922/)), and 24 weeks of patch beat 8 weeks at week 24 (31.6 % against 20.3 % point prevalence, n = 568) but the gap mostly closed by week 52 ([Schnoll et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-152-3-201002020-00005)). For simulated comparisons between fixed and adaptive arms, published effect sizes for personalised support are modest: 16.4 % against 10.0 % validated abstinence at 26 weeks for a just-in-time adaptive intervention in 454 low-income smokers (aOR 1.81, 1.03 to 3.18) ([Hébert et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2025.26691)), RR 1.54 for automated texting across 13 studies and 14,133 participants, and RR 1.00 for smartphone apps against lower-intensity support ([Whittaker et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD006611.pub5)). A relapse branch back to cigarettes also has support in dual users: among 380 smokers and dual users followed for up to 2 years, 83 % of dual users who moved to a single product moved to cigarettes ([Shafie-Khorassani et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/ntr/ntac207)), and non-daily vaping raised smoking relapse after a month of abstinence (aHR 2.82) in a 1,255-person US panel ([Cho et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwaf270)), although a 1,686-person PATH analysis found no association ([Sun et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/ntr/ntae201)).

## Morning latency and night waking carry most of the observable risk

Time to first use after waking is the strongest single self-report predictor of relapse in smokers. In Wisconsin cessation trials, **median days to relapse were 210 when the first cigarette came more than 60 min after waking (n = 95), 94 at 31 to 60 min (n = 154), 21 at 5 to 30 min (n = 501) and 7 within 5 min (n = 320)**, with median days to first lapse of 10, 10, 3 and 1; in the ITC survey, quit success ran from 35 % to 18 % across the same bands ([Baker et al. 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2933747/)). Time to first cigarette correlated only r = .32 with smoking rate and r < .14 with week-1 withdrawal severity or trajectory, so dependence and withdrawal sensitivity behave as largely separate traits. Each point of the Heaviness of Smoking Index cut 6-month abstinence odds (OR 0.84, 0.78 to 0.89, n = 2,535) ([Taggar et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13011-015-0011-8)), HSI was stable over 3 years (test-retest r = .72) ([Borland et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1093/ntr/ntq038)), and waking at night to smoke lowered 26-week abstinence (aOR 0.77, 0.62 to 0.96) and shortened time to relapse from 56 to 38.5 days in 2,312 smokers ([Bover et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18199277/)). The vaper evidence is cross-sectional or short: first vape within 5 min of waking carried OR 4.0 (2.1 to 7.7) for feeling very addicted among 832 ITC England vapers ([Lohner et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.16162)); occasional against daily use gave aOR 5.93 (3.11 to 11.30) for having quit at 6 months among 844 US vapers ([Sakhuja et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pmedr.2025.103197)); and in 311 young app users, low confidence, weak intention, pod depletion time, time to first vape, past-month alcohol, vaping frequency and mood trend were the top relapse features, with a C-index of 0.63 to 0.69 ([Kundu et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1093/ntr/ntag137)). Vapers score lower on dependence than they did as smokers (mean PSECDI 8.1 against a retrospective 14.5, n = 3,609) ([Foulds et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1093/ntr/ntu204)), while the LSBU sample averaged 12.05 ([Dawkins et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.14271)). More stable daily use signals higher dependence (PSECDI variance explained R² = 0.53 by within-person use dynamics in 116 dual users) ([Buu et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1093/ntr/ntab050)).

The metabolite ratio matters for dosing behaviour more than for outcome with a self-titrated product. Under placebo, slow and normal metabolisers quit at similar rates (17.2 % against 18.6 % at end of treatment, n = 1,246, cut-point 0.31) ([Lerman et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4480925/)), and a 9-RCT meta-analysis found no metaboliser effect with titratable NRT (OR 1.04, 0.95 to 1.14) against OR 0.81 (0.68 to 0.96) with fixed-dose NRT ([Xie et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2026.113294)); faster metabolisers took more daily puffs and larger total puff volume in 352 smokers ([Chen et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2018.06.003)). Affective and psychiatric traits carry modest effects that act through craving and volatility: past major depression OR 0.81 (0.67 to 0.97) for long-term abstinence across 38 studies ([Hitsman et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.12009)); anxiety diagnoses ORs of 0.65 to 0.74 in 1,504 smokers, with no benefit from pharmacotherapy ([Piper et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1360-0443.2010.03173.x)); and ADHD hyperactive-impulsive symptoms OR 0.40 (0.19 to 0.82) in a small subgroup of 583 smokers ([Covey et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1080/14622200802443536)). Alcohol produces a different kind of difficult person: binge drinkers lapsed faster (HR 1.42, 1.15 to 1.74) among 1,504 smokers ([Cook et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0029931)), and among 236 heavy-drinking smokers 41.5 % of first lapses occurred while drinking, drinking days carried almost 4 times the lapse risk, and alcohol-involved lapsers had lower tobacco dependence ([Kahler et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1093/ntr/ntq083)). Sex effects reverse between products: women quit smoking less often with patch (6-month patch OR 1.61 against 2.20 in men across 14 trials, n = 6,250) ([Perkins and Scott 2008](https://doi.org/10.1080/14622200802097506)), but quit vaping more often (18 % against 11 %, OR 1.88) in the single prospective vaping cohort ([Sakhuja et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pmedr.2025.103197)). Lower social grade halved quit-attempt success (11.4 % against 20.4 %) in 2,983 English attempters ([Kotz and West 2009](https://doi.org/10.1136/tc.2008.025981)).

A device that logs only puff times and durations can reconstruct proxies for most of the strongest predictors: first puff after the longest nightly gap (without wake time it confounds late waking with late use), puffs that split the habitual sleep gap, puffs/day and puff-seconds, day-to-day stability, evening and weekend clustering, abrupt within-day bursts, and the change in puff count and duration after each strength step. It cannot see psychiatric history, alcohol, sex, socioeconomic position, self-efficacy or craving. Device counts are valid but run about twice self-report (repeated-measures r = 0.49 in 18 young adults over 30 days) ([Li et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1093/ntr/ntaa205)), and self-reported puffs correlated only r = 0.37 with observed puffs over 4 h ([Dowd et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13396144/)). Passive sensing has flagged lapses ahead of time in smokers with extra sensors (a risk estimate peaking on average 44 min before a lapse in 92 quitters) ([Ullah et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1145/3550308)), but no published study predicts vaping lapse from device puff logs alone, and none tests whether a person's compensation after a cut predicts later relapse.

## Structural changes the evidence implies

The first change concerns what C represents. The published disposition has a distribution phase of about 8 min that carries a large share of the early fall, and a three-compartment smoker fit puts 40 % of the AUC in phases of 7 and 57 min, so a two-compartment (or an effect-site) formulation would stop the model from overstating concentration during the 20 to 30 min after a session. Each synthetic person's clearance would be drawn from a covariate mixture (CYP2A6 activity group or metabolite-ratio quartile, sex, oestrogen-containing contraception) rather than from a single σ of 0.2, giving the slow-metaboliser tail that published quartiles place near 164 min ([Benowitz et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4892970/)). Delivery would scale with puff duration at an exponent near 1 to 1.3, not 0.7, with the functional form flagged as unidentified beyond three machine-measured durations.

The second change separates the single tolerance state into the three clocks the evidence resolves: a fast acute-tolerance state (half-life about 35 min, resetting overnight), the chronic state S that drives the withdrawal syndrome (τ about 5 d, range 3 to 8 d), and a slow state (receptor normalisation or conditioned craving, τ of weeks to months) that drives craving and relapse vulnerability. Craving decay would then be decoupled from the tolerance constant, since `puffsim.py` currently sets both from one draw. Withdrawal itself splits into a craving component that saturates within about 3 h of a deficit and an affective syndrome that builds with a lag of about 12 to 24 h and peaks on days 1 to 3, and the deficit could be computed on a log or Emax scale of C, given receptor occupancy half-saturates at under 1 ng/mL. Person-level trajectory types (moderate, high craving, affective, late-rising or volatile) would be drawn in rough published proportions, because pharmacotherapy lowers severity without changing slope or volatility and the same is plausible for a gentle taper.

The third change replaces the relapse function. The evidence supports a discrete-time logistic hazard in within-person standardised craving with a slope of about 0.64 per SD (range 0.27 to 1.10), a term for the 3-day rise in craving, short-lived cue terms (alcohol, others vaping or smoking, stress rising over hours), and no hard threshold. Lapse and relapse become two linked processes, with relapse emerging after repeated lapses (median 5 over about 13 days in smokers) and each lapse raising the next lapse's hazard. A separate clock starts when a synthetic person reaches zero, because every drug trial shows its steepest losses just after treatment ends, and the background hazard decays with time since that point rather than staying at 0.0003 per day throughout. The population curve h(t) ≈ 0.40/(t + 1)^1.05 per day, and the 13 to 20 % trial abstinence anchors, then serve as calibration targets that emerge from heterogeneity rather than as per-person inputs. A relapse branch back to cigarettes is supported for dual users and ex-smokers.

The fourth change gives compensation its own state. In the current code compensation is a gain on W and fades as S catches up with C; in vapers compensation builds over the first 5 days and shows no fade within a week, and self-selected reducers hold cotinine flat for 8 to 12 months. A separate compensation state with an onset time constant of roughly 1 to 3 days (derived from qualitative results) and no decay at moderate strengths would match that, with a power-law response to the strength ratio (ε drawn around a median 0.35, IQR 0.27 to 0.47, truncated near −0.05 and 0.75, from the internal LSBU reanalysis) and a cap near ×2.2. Any fade would be confined, by analogy with very low nicotine cigarettes, to very low absolute strengths, and that analogy is unsourced for vaping. Compensation strength would correlate with dependence (a 42 % larger duration response at moderate to high dependence in pod users).

The fifth change rebuilds the topography generator as nested levels (person, day, session with gaps of minutes, 60 s bouts, puffs), with overdispersed bout sizes that put 37 to 47 % of bouts at a single puff, lognormal in-bout gaps with a median near 13 s and σ(ln) near 0.7 that shorten in long bouts, lognormal puff durations with CV near 0.5 around a device-class median, daily multipliers with CVs of roughly 0.2 to 0.6, a small negative weekend effect with a later-shifted day, a device-adoption period of 1 to 3 weeks, and night waking as a trait present in about 10 % of people who wake on most nights. Simulated logs would then be scored with the same 60 s rule used in the literature so that synthetic and published bout statistics are comparable.

The sixth change correlates the person-level traits. Morning latency, night use, puffs/day and use stability load on one dependence factor; withdrawal and craving sensitivity correlate only weakly with it (r < .14 in smokers); affective vulnerability raises craving volatility; and an alcohol or social cue trait sits roughly independent of, or opposite to, physiological dependence. The simulated population of taper-seekers would also skew toward the "prefer gradual" profile, whose published success is about half that of people who prefer to stop abruptly.

## What the literature cannot supply, and the pilot must measure

The parameters that stay unknown after this review are the ones specific to Wane's situation: a vaper reducing nicotine over months while keeping the device. No publication gives vaper-specific withdrawal or craving responses to graded strength steps, compensation beyond one week of an imposed cut or at strengths below about 3 mg/mL, the functional form linking cut size to withdrawal, the floor constant, the within-person spread of daily craving needed to set the hazard slope, lapse-to-relapse progression when nicotine remains available, relapse hazards after reaching zero in ex-vapers, the between-person distribution of clearance in vapers, the delivery-duration curve for the pilot hardware, or any link between device-observed features and vaping relapse. ## Conclusion

The review changes what kind of model `puffsim.py` needs more than it changes individual numbers. The guesses that matter most for ranking taper strategies are not the central half-life or tolerance constant, which hold up, but the assumptions that make the simulator smooth and optimistic: a narrow spread of clearance, a sub-linear delivery curve, compensation that fades on its own, bouts that never contain a single puff, day-to-day noise a third of what device logs show, and a relapse switch whose steepness no pooled estimate supports. Each of these flatters any controller that reacts to the average person, and each hides exactly the irregular, heavy-tailed users on whom Wane's engine is meant to be judged.

The evidence also shifts where the uncertainty lives. Pharmacokinetics and topography are now well enough bounded by published data to stop being guesses; the unresolved questions are behavioural and specific to a taper with the device always at hand: how much compensation persists over months, how withdrawal and craving respond to each step, and how lapses turn into relapse when nicotine never disappears. Those are the quantities that decide whether one simulated taper beats another, and they can only come from the pilot's own logs.
